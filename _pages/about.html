New paper · Nov 2026

How we hear what things are made of

{% assign pnas = site.data.publications | where: "year", 2026 | where: "theme", "perception" | first %}

PNAS · 2026 · first author

{{ pnas.title }}

{{ pnas.authors | replace: "V. Agarwal", "V. Agarwal" }}

When two objects collide, you can hear what they're made of and how heavy they are, even though the sound blends contributions from both. We recorded the acoustic "fingerprints" of hundreds of everyday objects, built a generative model from their statistics, and used it to synthesize controlled collisions. Listeners judged these properties accurately. But when both objects varied, their judgments got worse whenever the synthetic resonances departed from real-world statistics. The brain appears to carry an internal model of how objects sound, and it uses that model to split a sound into its physical causes.

The path

From hearing physics to building agents

The common thread is building models of how the world works, testing them hard against real data, and then putting them to work. I've done that first in human perception, then in biology, and now with agents that act on the world.

  1. 2026 – now

    Agents that act · Composio

    Founding Head of Forward Deployed Engineering. More above ↑

  2. 2024 – 2026

    AI that does science · Biostate AI & K‑Dense

    At Biostate AI I contributed to foundation models of biology (GeneJepa, Revive-Flow) and to the agents that grew into K‑Dense: K‑Dense Analyst for automated scientific analysis, and later Scientific Agent Skills, an open-source skills library for research agents with 50k+ GitHub stars. I was first author on work in which a guided multi-agent AI system invented transcriptomic aging clocks that are accurate and know when their own predictions are uncertain, with collaborators including David Sinclair's lab at Harvard Medical School.

  3. 2018 – 2025

    How the brain hears physics · MIT

    PhD at MIT, from 2020 with Josh McDermott in the Laboratory for Computational Audition (and before that in ocean acoustics with Nicholas Makris). I built differentiable, physics-based synthesizers for impacts, scrapes and rolls, and used them to ask what listeners actually infer from sound. The answer is a kind of auditory intuitive physics. I was a major contributor to the audio in the ThreeDWorld simulator, whose paper cites my scraping-and-rolling synthesis model as its first reference. Along the way I also consulted for NVIDIA, and worked on generative spatial audio at Meta Reality Labs.

  4. 2013 – 2018

    Acoustics and music · IIT Bombay

    B.Tech + M.Tech in Mechanical Engineering, with a thesis on the acoustics of engineered foams. Research internships at CNRS Marseille (gesture-controlled sound synthesis) and Simon Fraser University (machine transcription of electronic music).
